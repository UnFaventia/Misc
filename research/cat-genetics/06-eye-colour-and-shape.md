## At a glance

- **Apart from blue, cat eye-colour genetics haven't been studied.** Most "coat-linked" eye colours are decades of breeder selection, not genetic linkage. Simpson's 1903 argument for green-eyed silvers shows the selection happening in real time.
- **The genuine genetic links:**
  - colourpoint (TYR `cs`) → blue
  - albinism → pale blue/lilac/pink
  - Dominant White (`Wd`) and white spotting (`Ws`, when there's white around the eyes) → blue or odd eyes
  - **DBE (PAX3)** → blue regardless of coat
  - sepia and mink *bias* the range but don't fix it
- **Eye colour is a continuum,** not a set of categories: green ↔ hazel ↔ yellow ↔ amber ↔ orange ↔ copper, plus blue ↔ aqua. Pedigree eyes are more intense; randombred eyes are paler and more mixed.
- **Every kitten is born blue.** The adult colour starts coming in at about **6 weeks**.
- **For the calculator:** eye colour should be a *range* by genotype, with an optional "pedigree standard" overlay. The portrait already fills each eye separately, so odd eyes are cheap to add.

### Breed conventions vs genetics

- **Sepia (Burmese)** isn't inextricably linked to gold. Western Burmese were selected for gold because it contrasted best with the dark brown coat.
  - Sepia cats can also have yellow, orange, green or greenish-blue eyes, more often in native Thai cats.
  - European Burmese standard: "any shade of yellow from chartreuse to amber, golden yellow preferred". TICA American Burmese standard: gold.
- **Mink (Tonkinese):** the preferred colour is a turquoise between Siamese and pedigree Burmese. Naturally occurring mink-pattern cats can have almost any colour. Most of the range was bred out of the Burmese parent early on.
- **Solid, tabby, tortie, etc.:** eye colour was chosen by breeders to harmonise with the coat and written into the standards. It isn't genetically linked.
- **Silver (inhibitor):** pedigree silvers' green eyes (colourpoints aside) are a deliberate early choice, not linkage. Simpson, 1903:
  > "…to my mind the bright emerald green eye is the ideal for a silver cat. I have seen very fine amber eyes which could not fail to attract admiration; but if these are admitted, then all sorts of eyes, not amber but wishy-washy yellow, will be the inevitable result… Therefore it is best to set up a standard, and I think it is becoming an almost undisputed fact that silver cats of perfect type should have green eyes, and by green let it be understood that the deeper the tone the better…"

  She also records the self conventions of her day: blue eyes for whites, orange for blacks and blues. **Pewter** (orange-eyed shaded silver) was the *original* eye colour of the breed, until it fell out of favour in the 1890s (see [03](03-silver-golden-shading.md)).

## Odd eyes (heterochromia iridium)

- **Causes:** genetic, congenital (a developmental defect), or acquired (illness, injury, medication). In the cat fancy, "odd-eyed" means **one blue + one other colour**.
- **Where it's common:** most common in **Wd** whites. Pedigree odd-eyed whites are blue + orange/amber; randombreds can be blue + yellow, green or brown.
  - Heterochromia from birth is also common in horses (blue/brown) and some dog breeds (blue/brown), and uncommon in humans.
- **Odd eye-shine:** one eye has a **tapetum lucidum** (green shine in the dark) and the other lacks it (red shine). It shows up in headlights or flash photos.
- **Mechanism:** there's no "odd-eye gene". White spotting and dominant white block pigment production in the embryo, including in the iris.
  - If some melanocytes stay active around one eye, that eye gets colour.
  - Melanocyte activity sets how far the eye goes from blue-tinged to fully coloured, and from pale to intense.
  - The colour can **deepen over the cat's lifetime**, gradually replacing the blue.
- **In coloured cats** (rare):
  - congenital abnormality, mosaicism (merged embryos), or a somatic mutation (a "birthmark") — **not hereditary**
  - a white-spotting gene that affected the eye but not the fur
  - in torties, X-inactivation mosaicism may extend to the eyes (e.g. a tortie with one yellow and one blue eye)
  - eye injury or illness — not heritable

## Sectoral heterochromia / dichroic eyes

- **What it is:** distinct areas of one iris with different melanocyte numbers or activity. The result is a **pie slice** or a **coloured halo**, e.g. a yellow ring around a green iris.
- It's a show fault, "but can be very attractive".
- **In white cats:** reported as a brown sector in a blue or green eye, where the white gene affected only part of the iris. When both eyes have it, they can be mirror images. Sometimes called "weird-eyed" cats.

## Albinism

- **Where it shows up:** albinism appears in the Siamese and closely related Oriental breeds **through inbreeding**.
- **Pangur** (albino Oriental Longhair, photos by Greer Stothers, 2013):
  - The eyes look faded lilac-blue. Close up, the pupil is dull red, and the "lilac" is pale blue refraction from the lens over a pinkish pupil.
  - **Very photosensitive.**
  - Pangur's parents are half-siblings with the same father.
- **Blue vs pink eyes:** true albinos lack melanin entirely, and the iris is pale pinkish-white. Blue refraction usually gives a bluish hue, so most are **blue-eyed albinos**. Very rarely the transparent structures are colourless, giving a **pink-eyed albino**.
  - Pink eyes in cats were once thought impossible, but several pink-eyed albinos have been reported recently.
  - Albinism has been recorded in the **Bengal** and its wild parent, the **Asian Leopard Cat**.
- **Calculator:** `c/c` is modelled as one albino allele, and the note says "pale pink-blue eyes". The mocha diagram writes the albino allele as **`ca`**.
  - *Lead to verify:* cat genetics often lists two albino alleles, blue-eyed `ca` and pink-eyed `c`. If both are real, blue-eyed vs pink-eyed albino could be genotype-driven rather than a single outcome.

## Pink-eyed dilution

- **What it is:** the second kind of mammalian dilution, separate from the blue dilution (`d`) cats normally have. It gives a **bluish-tan/fawn** coat and depigments the eye to pink or ruby.
- **Reliably reported once** (Todd 1961): a pink-eyed female with a light tan coat.
  - Her owner's elderly neighbour stabbed her and her **seven week-old kittens** to death, all of whom showed the same depigmentation as their mother. He blamed the owner's fruit tree for the rat infestation in his home.
  - None survived, so the mutation was lost.
- **Possible reappearance:** in the **Donskoy**: pale tan cats with yellow eyes whose pupils reflect pink (see [10](10-rare-and-future.md)). The eye diagram describes "Pink-Eyed Dilution" as a red pupil, a yellow/gold iris, and a bluish-beige coat.
- **Calculator:** ❌. It would be a new recessive locus.

## Chediak-Higashi syndrome

- Pale irises and under-pigmented retinas, together with a pale coat. Seen in **Blue Smoke Persians**. See [11](11-health-and-welfare.md).

## Source quirks & open questions

- The source says "hazel (the normal colour of temperate climate **white** cats)". The earlier paragraph says temperate **wild** cats have hazel eyes, so "white" is probably a typo for "wild".
- **Pink-eyed dilution coat:** the diagram says "bluish-beige", the prose says "bluish-tan/fawn", and the Donskoy entry says "pale tan". These are probably the same phenotype described three ways.
- **Albino allele:** the mocha diagram's `ca` vs the calculator's `c`. Are these one allele or two?
- **Chinchilla Turquoise:** is it the same colour as the "black-rimmed sea-green" chinchilla eye, or a distinct one?
- **Mocha eye colour** isn't covered in these notes. The calculator's "aqua" for mocha needs a source.
